# Observation of Vinen Turbulence during Far-from-Equilibrium Bose-Einstein Condensation
Sebastian J. Morris$^{*}$, Martin Gazo, Simon M. Fischer, Haoyu Zhang, Christopher J. Ho$^{\dagger}$, Nigel R. Cooper, Christoph Eigen, and Zoran Hadzibabic
*Cavendish Laboratory, University of Cambridge, J. J. Thomson Avenue, Cambridge CB3 0US, United Kingdom*
(Received 8 May 2026; revised 25 June 2026; accepted 10 September 2026; published 5 November 2026)
Relaxation of far-from-equilibrium quantum fluids, intimately related to the emergence of long-range order, is theoretically associated with the decay of a turbulent isotropic tangle of vortex lines. We observe and study such decaying quantum turbulence in a homogeneous 3D atomic Bose gas. Using matter-wave techniques to magnify the gas density distribution, and then imaging a thin slice of the magnified cloud, we observe imprints of randomly oriented vortex lines and measure the vortex-line-length density $\mathcal{L}$. The observed decay of $\mathcal{L}$ agrees with the prediction for Vinen “ultraquantum” turbulence. Although our weakly interacting gases are highly compressible, their large-scale dynamics are consistent with the behavior of an incompressible hydrodynamic fluid, with the decay of $\mathcal{L}$ not depending on the strength of the interatomic interactions and being similar to that in the strongly interacting superfluid helium.
DOI: 10.1103/lywd-gjg9
A striking feature of quantum fluids is their irrotational flow, which leads to a quantized circulation that establishes topological defects in the form of vortex lines [1–4]. The interactions and reconnections of quantized vortices are at the heart of a rich phenomenology of far-from-equilibrium dynamics, relevant across many fields and length scales [5–11]. Central to these dynamics is the phenomenon of quantum turbulence, which has long been a cornerstone of research in superfluid helium [12–16].
The regime of Vinen or ultraquantum turbulence [13,16] corresponds to the decay of a random vortex tangle [see Fig. 1(a)] without any large-scale (classical) flow. Such turbulence has been theoretically linked to the dynamics of Kibble’s strings in cosmology [8] and predicted to play a key role in far-from-equilibrium Bose-Einstein condensation (the emergence of long-range coherence) [24–30]. Quantitatively, it is characterized by the vortex-line-length density $\mathcal{L}$, i.e., the total length of vortex lines per unit volume. where $B$ is a constant with units of the quantum of circulation, $2\pi\hbar/m$, with $m$ being the particle mass [13,25]. Individual vortex lines have also been visualized using tracer particles, but such tracers are large compared to the vortex core and can modify the vortex dynamics [31–34].
Ultracold atomic gases provide an excellent setting for studies of both driven and freely decaying turbulence [35–51]. In many situations, these systems also allow direct imaging of vortices [35,37,39–41,43,46,52–64], but a quantitative study of isotropic 3D vortex tangles has remained a challenge. Recently, a study of far-from-equilibrium condensation in a homogeneous gas [50] revealed dynamics of the coherence length $\ell$ consistent with Eq. (1), assuming that $\ell$ is set by the typical vortex-line separation [13], so $\ell \propto \mathcal{L}^{-1/2}$. This study was based on measurements of the gas momentum distribution, which is sensitive to the presence of vortices but does not directly reveal them, and the observed dynamics of $\ell$ were also consistent with wave-based theories [65–68].
In this Letter, we directly observe Vinen turbulence, during far-from-equilibrium condensation in a homogeneous 3D atomic gas. To reveal imprints of the randomly oriented vortex lines, we magnify the cloud [19–21] and then image just a thin slice of it [22]; see Fig. 1(b). We measure the vortex-line-length density $\mathcal{L}$, observe a nonlinear decay of $\mathcal{L}$ in accordance with Eq. (1), and show that the prefactor $B$ does not depend on the strength of the (weak) interparticle interactions. As in the strongly interacting superfluid helium [69], we find $B \approx \hbar/m$.
In our experiments, we prepare an isolated Bose gas in a far-from-equilibrium initial state by starting with a Bose-Einstein condensate and then perturbing it, as in Refs. [50,70]. Our $^{39}\text{K}$ gas is in the lowest hyperfine state, trapped in a cylindrical optical box [17,18] of volume $V \approx 9 \times 10^4 \mu\text{m}^3$ [diameter $47(2)\mu\text{m}$ $\mu\text{m}$ and length $52(2)\mu\text{m}$], and has density $n \approx 2.2 \mu\text{m}^{-3}$, corresponding to the critical temperature for condensation $T_c \approx 70\text{ nK}$. The initial state is incoherent but low energy, with energy per particle $\approx k_B \times 20\text{ nK}$, corresponding to the equilibrium condensed fraction $\eta \approx 0.5$ [50]. We tune the interactions in the gas, characterized by the $s$-wave scattering length $a$, using the Feshbach resonance at $402.7\text{ G}$ [71]; the initial incoherent state is prepared at $a = 0$ [72] and we initiate the gas relaxation by turning on the interactions, at time $t = 0$. In the interacting gas, the (equilibrium) radius of the vortex-line core is set by the healing length, $\xi = 1/\sqrt{8\pi na}$, of order $1\mu\text{m}$.
To observe vortex imprints, we switch off the box trap and the interactions ($a \to 0$) and magnify the cloud [19–21] in the $x\text{-}y$ plane by a factor $M \approx 3.5$ [see Fig. 1(b)]; as in Refs. [19–21,73–75], we use a pulsed harmonic potential as a matter-wave lens [76]. We then image just a central slice of the cloud [22], of thickness $d \approx 15\mu\text{m}$ [77]. The vortex lines that cross this slice appear as high-contrast dips (dark lines) in the imaged 2D density $n_s$. Theoretically, the *in situ* FWHM of the density depletion due to a vortex core is $\approx 3\xi$ [23], while the apparent vortex-core diameter in our images ($\approx 15\mu\text{m}$) is slightly larger than $3M\xi$, both due to our imaging resolution ($\approx 5\mu\text{m}$) and because the free expansion along $z$ during the $19\text{ ms}$ of $x\text{-}y$ magnification leads to some blurring.
In Fig. 2(a), we show characteristic slice images for different relaxation times, with $a$ set to $430a_0$ (where $a_0$ is the Bohr radius). At early times, we observe only small-scale density fluctuations, while at long times (here $t \gtrsim 80\text{ ms}$) we observe well-defined and well-separated vortex imprints.
For comparison, in Fig. 2(b) we show the corresponding evolution of $\ell^2(t)$, which shows universal coarsening, given by $d\ell^2/dt \approx 3.4\hbar/m$ [50], once $\ell$ significantly exceeds $\xi$ and until it becomes comparable to the system size; as in Ref. [50], we obtain $\ell(t)$ from momentum-distribution measurements and normalize it such that $\ell^3 = V$ for an equilibrium condensate.

For the quantitative analysis of our data, we employ the general mathematical result for an isotropic random tangle of lines [79],
$$\mathcal{L} = 4 N_{\text{i}} / S, \eqno(2)$$
where $N_{\text{i}}$ is the number of intersections of lines with a subvolume of surface area $S$. In our case, $N_{\text{i}}$ is the number of distinct vortex imprints, and $S$ is the *in situ* surface area corresponding to the region where we count them [80].
We define the image area covered by the imprints by $n_s < \alpha \langle n_s \rangle$, where $\langle n_s \rangle$ is the average $n_s$, and set the cutoff $\alpha = 0.5$ to capture the high-contrast depletions associated with the vortices while excluding other density variations (including noise). We then count $N_{\text{i}}$ by grouping the imprint pixels into the same cluster when they are separated by $\leq 4$ pixels $\approx 10 \text{ }\mu\text{m}$ [81].
![Figure 4](fig4.png)
FIG. 4. Counting distinct vortex imprints. As expected, the average number $N_{\text{i}}$ of distinct vortex imprints counted by our algorithm is proportional to the average fractional area $A$ that they cover [see Fig. 3(a)].
More intuitively, $\mathcal{L}$ is proportional to the average fractional area $A$ covered by the imprints: $\mathcal{L} \approx A / (wd)$, where $w$ is the average imprint width. We show in Fig. 4 that our $N_{\text{i}}$, and hence $\mathcal{L}$ extracted according to Eq. (2), is indeed proportional to $A$. Then, Eq. (2) provides an absolute calibration of $\mathcal{L}/A$ in a way that does not depend on the vortex-core size and allows direct comparison of the dynamics at different $a$ [82].
In Fig. 5(a), we plot the extracted $\mathcal{L}(t)$ for $a = 430 a_0$ and $t > 80 \text{ ms}$. At very long times (very small $\mathcal{L}$), the data are described well by a simple exponential decay (dashed line), with a time constant $\approx 300 \text{ ms}$; such one-body decay is suggestive of individual vortices annihilating on the trap walls with their images [83]. However, at earlier times we observe a decay faster than the exponential one, which we attribute to the vortex-vortex interactions that drive Vinen turbulence. Augmenting Eq. (1) to include one-body decay, we have
$$\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{dt} = -B \mathcal{L}^2 - \frac{\mathcal{L}}{\tau}, \eqno(3)$$

Accounting for the difference in the particle mass, our $B$ is similar to that observed in superfluid $^4\text{He}$ in the low-temperature regime, where $B \sim \hbar/m_{\text{He}}$ [13,14,69], despite the large differences between the microscopics of the two systems (even in equilibrium) and the fact that in our case the typical ratio of the vortex-line separation $\mathcal{L}^{-1/2}$ and the vortex-core size is smaller by about 5 orders of magnitude. Numerical simulations of the Gross-Pitaevskii equation also give $B \sim \hbar/m$ [28,29] (see also Refs. [85-87]).
In conclusion, we have observed random vortex lines during far-from-equilibrium Bose-Einstein condensation in an isolated homogeneous 3D gas and shown their decay to be consistent with Vinen turbulence. We observe well-defined vortices and their universal decay in the regime of gas relaxation when the coherence length is already much larger than the vortex-core size; in the future, it would be interesting to also study the earlier stages of relaxation and the expected transition from wave to vortex-dominated turbulence [4]. Initial state—To prepare our far-from-equilibrium initial state, we follow Ref. [70]. We start with a quasipure condensate, turn off the interactions, and drive the noninteracting gas for $\approx 1\text{ s}$ along the box axis using a time-varying force $F \cos(\omega_s t)$, where $\omega_s / (2\pi) = 10 \text{ Hz}$ and $F/m \approx 0.1 \text{ ms}^{-2}$, created by a magnetic field gradient. In the absence of interactions, weak optical disorder within the box scatters the atoms into transverse directions, so once we stop driving and wait for $\approx 1\text{ s}$, the state is essentially isotropic. Our linear driving does not introduce angular momentum into the gas, and the isotropic initial state has no net vorticity or any other large-scale flow.
