# Haag's Theorem: The Strange Problem at the Heart of Quantum Field Theory

Bill Giannakopoulos Following

8 min read · 4 days ago

![](_page_0_Picture_10.jpeg)

Quantum field theory is among the most successful theories ever constructed. It underlies our understanding of particles and forces and has produced predictions of extraordinary precision.

Yet buried within its foundations is a peculiar result known as Haag's theorem.

![](_page_0_Picture_9.jpeg)

(1)

The theorem does not say that quantum field theory is wrong. Nor does it invalidate its remarkable experimental successes. Instead, it tells us that one of the most natural ways of imagining how interactions work in quantum field theory cannot literally be correct.

To understand why, we need only a few basic ideas.

#### What Is a Hilbert Space?

Quantum mechanics does not describe the world simply by assigning particles definite positions and velocities.

Instead, it describes the possible states of a quantum system within a mathematical structure called a Hilbert space.

The easiest way to imagine a Hilbert space is as the complete landscape of possible states available to a quantum system.

As the system changes, its state moves through this landscape.

In ordinary quantum mechanics, we generally imagine that this evolution occurs within the same overall Hilbert-space framework.

This leads to another important concept: unitarity.

#### What Does Unitary Mean?

Unitary evolution means, roughly speaking, that a quantum system can change while preserving the quantum information contained within it.

The state may evolve dramatically, but the transformation does not simply destroy information. In principle, the evolution remains reversible.

This produces a powerful picture of quantum reality.

There is a space of possible states, and physical evolution carries the system from one state to another while preserving the underlying quantum structure.

For relatively simple quantum systems, this works beautifully.

But quantum field theory introduces a complication.

#### From Particles to Fields

Quantum field theory does not fundamentally begin with individual particles.

It begins with fields.

An electron can be understood as an excitation of an electron field. A photon is an excitation of the electromagnetic field.

And unlike a simple quantum system, a quantum field has, in the idealized theory, infinitely many degrees of freedom.

That difference turns out to matter enormously.

With infinitely many degrees of freedom, it becomes possible to have different realizations of the same basic quantum relationships that cannot be connected by an ordinary unitary transformation.

This possibility lies at the heart of Haag's theorem.

### Start With a World Without Interactions

The easiest quantum fields to describe are free fields.

A free field is an idealization in which the excitations of the field do not interact.

Such theories are mathematically manageable. We can identify their particles, describe their possible states and define their vacuum — the lowest-energy state of the field.

But the real universe is obviously not made from non-interacting fields.

Electrons interact with electromagnetic fields. Quarks interact through gluon fields. Particles scatter, decay, disappear and appear.

Physicists therefore need to describe interactions.

The natural solution is ingenious:

Start with the theory without interactions and then introduce the interactions.

This is the basic intuition behind what physicists call the interaction picture.

#### The Interaction Picture

The interaction picture begins with a free theory that we understand well.

Interactions are then introduced, and their effects are calculated as modifications of that simpler theory.

This approach lies behind perturbation theory and much of the familiar machinery of Feynman diagrams.

Conceptually, it sounds completely reasonable:

First describe what the particles are when they are free. Then calculate what happens when they interact.

But hidden inside this procedure is an important assumption.

It assumes that the free theory and the interacting theory can be related in the appropriate way by a unitary transformation.

In other words, the interacting world should in some sense be reachable from the free world without abandoning the underlying quantum representation.

And this is where Rudolf Haag discovered the problem.

#### Haag's Theorem

In the 1950s, Haag showed that, under the usual assumptions of relativistic quantum field theory, a genuinely interacting field cannot simply be related to the corresponding free field by the unitary transformation required by the naive interaction picture.

The free theory and interacting theory can belong to what mathematicians call unitarily inequivalent representations.

That phrase sounds forbidding, but the central idea is surprisingly simple.

The free theory and the interacting theory cannot always be regarded as merely different states or descriptions within one common quantum representation, connected by an ordinary unitary transformation.

Something deeper has changed.

#### What Does "Unitarily Inequivalent" Mean?

It is tempting to say that the free and interacting theories simply live in different Hilbert spaces.

That captures part of the intuition, but it is not quite mathematically accurate.

Two Hilbert spaces can look identical as abstract mathematical structures while representing physics in fundamentally different ways.

Imagine two identical theatres.

The buildings may have exactly the same architecture. But suppose the rules governing everything that can happen on the two stages are fundamentally different.

There may then be no transformation that takes every actor, movement and relationship on one stage and converts them perfectly into those of the other.

The stages are structurally alike, but the worlds being represented upon them are not equivalent.

Something similar happens in quantum field theory.

The crucial issue is therefore not simply whether there is one Hilbert space or two. It is how the physical world is represented within that mathematical space.

Haag's theorem tells us that the free and interacting representations cannot generally be connected in the straightforward unitary way suggested by the interaction picture.

#### Why Doesn't This Destroy Quantum Field Theory?

At first sight this sounds disastrous.

If the interaction picture cannot literally exist in the way its simplest interpretation suggests, why do calculations based upon it work so extraordinarily well?

Because practical quantum field theory is more sophisticated than that simple picture.

Physicists developed techniques called regularization and renormalization.

Rather than immediately attempting to deal with an idealized field possessing infinitely many degrees of freedom at every possible scale, calculations are temporarily restricted or reorganized.

The theory is then handled in such a way that meaningful physical predictions can be extracted.

And those predictions work spectacularly well.

Haag's theorem therefore does not tell us that the calculations of quantum field theory are wrong.

It tells us that we should not take the simple conceptual story behind those calculations too literally.

The mathematics used to obtain predictions works, while the intuitive picture of simply taking a free quantum world and smoothly switching on interactions is more problematic.

#### Fock Space and the Meaning of a Particle

Another useful idea is Fock space.

Ordinary quantum mechanics can describe a fixed number of particles.

Quantum field theory must allow particles to appear and disappear.

A Fock space provides a framework capable of describing a vacuum, one particle, two particles, three particles and so forth.

For a free field, this construction is particularly clean.

But interactions complicate the picture.

The vacuum of an interacting theory is not simply an empty container waiting for particles to be placed inside it. It already reflects the structure of the interacting theory.

Likewise, what we mean by a particle becomes more subtle once interactions are present.

This is another indication that the transition from a free world to an interacting world is deeper than the simple language of "adding an interaction" might suggest.

#### The Vacuum Is Not Nothing

The word vacuum is itself misleading.

In everyday language, a vacuum means empty space.

In quantum field theory, the vacuum means the lowest-energy state of a particular theory.

Change the interactions and the structure of that lowest-energy state can change as well.

The free vacuum and the interacting vacuum therefore cannot automatically be treated as ordinary states related by a simple change of perspective.

The interaction reaches all the way down into what counts as the vacuum itself.

#### What Is a Representation?

This brings us to perhaps the most important idea.

Quantum theory contains basic mathematical relationships between physical quantities. These relationships can be described abstractly as an algebra.

A representation is a particular way of realizing those relationships within a Hilbert space.

In simpler quantum systems, the physically relevant representations are generally equivalent in the way we need them to be.

In quantum field theory, with infinitely many degrees of freedom, that uniqueness disappears.

There can be different physically meaningful representations that cannot be transformed into one another by a unitary transformation.

This is one of the great conceptual differences between ordinary quantum mechanics and quantum field theory.

It also tells us something important:

Hilbert space may not be the deepest level of the story.

#### Haag and Algebraic Quantum Field Theory

Haag himself became one of the major figures in an alternative approach known as algebraic quantum field theory.

Instead of beginning by declaring one particular Hilbert-space representation to be fundamental, algebraic quantum field theory gives greater prominence to the underlying relationships between physical observables.

Different Hilbert-space representations can then be understood as different ways in which this deeper algebraic structure is realized.

Conceptually, this reverses the usual order of thinking.

Rather than beginning with a Hilbert space and putting the physics inside it,

we can begin with the fundamental relationships and ask how those relationships can be represented.

This may seem like a subtle mathematical distinction.

Philosophically, however, it is intriguing.

#### Objects and Processes

Much of our ordinary thinking divides reality into objects and processes.

First there are things.

Then those things interact.

An electron exists, another particle exists, and then we ask what happens when they encounter one another.

The interaction picture seems to reflect this intuition remarkably well.

First construct the free particles. Then introduce the interactions between them.

Haag's theorem does not prove that this philosophical picture is wrong.

But it does show that its most straightforward mathematical realization fails in relativistic quantum field theory.

We cannot simply assume that the world of free objects and the world of interacting objects are two versions of the same quantum representation connected by ordinary unitary evolution.

That raises an interesting possibility.

Perhaps objects and interactions are not completely independent ingredients

#### of reality.

Perhaps what we call an object already depends upon the network of interactions in which it exists.

Instead of nature first supplying objects and then separately specifying how those objects interact, both might arise from a deeper quantum structure.

An object could then be understood as a relatively persistent pattern within that structure.

A process would be a transformation of that structure.

What appears to us as thing and change might therefore represent two aspects of something more fundamental.

This interpretation goes beyond Haag's theorem. The theorem does not prove that reality is fundamentally relational, nor does it prove that objects are really processes.

But it gives us a reason to question an assumption inherited from everyday experience:

that we can completely define what something is independently of how it relates to everything else.

Perhaps at the deepest level, what something is and what it does cannot be completely separated.

## What Haag's Theorem Really Tells Us

Haag's theorem is sometimes presented dramatically as showing that quantum field theory is mathematically inconsistent.

That is too strong.

It does not say that quantum field theory's predictions are invalid. It does not say that interactions cannot be described. And it does not make Feynman diagrams meaningless.

Its lesson is subtler and, in some ways, more interesting.

The apparently obvious idea that we can construct an interacting quantum world by beginning with a non-interacting quantum world and then simply switching on the interactions cannot be taken literally in the exact theory under Haag's assumptions.

Quantum field theory is telling us that the world of infinitely many degrees of freedom does not always fit comfortably inside the intuitions we inherited from simpler quantum mechanics.

There need not be one fixed quantum representation within which every physically relevant description can be connected to every other by unitary transformations.

And that leaves us with a deeper conceptual question.

Perhaps the difficulty is not merely that interactions are mathematically complicated.

Perhaps the distinction we begin with — things first, interactions second — is itself less fundamental than it appears.

Haag's theorem does not answer that question.

But it gives us a very good reason to ask it.
