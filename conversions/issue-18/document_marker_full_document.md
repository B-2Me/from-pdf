# Pseudo-chiral phonons from octupolar magnetic order

Ruairidh Sutcliffe <sup>ϕ</sup> , 1, [<sup>∗</sup>](#page-0-0) Kathleen Hart <sup>ϕ</sup> , 1, [†](#page-0-1) Swati Chaudhary <sup>ϕ</sup> , 2, [‡](#page-0-2) and Arun Paramekanti1, [§](#page-0-3)

<sup>1</sup>Department of Physics, University of Toronto, 60 St. George Street, Toronto, ON, M5S 1A7 Canada <sup>2</sup>The Institute for Solid State Physics, The University of Tokyo, Kashiwa, Chiba 277-8581, Japan

Motivated by the recent discovery of anomalously large magnetic response of chiral phonons in dipolar magnets, we introduce the concept of pseudo-chiral phonons which are shown to emerge in multipolar magnets. We consider Raman active quantum phonons, such as doublet E<sup>g</sup> phonons (dx2−y<sup>2</sup> , d3z2−r<sup>2</sup> ) in cubic crystals, which feature a symmetry-allowed linear coupling to local quadrupolar moments. We show that distinct multipolar orders can imprint distinct patterns of degeneracy breaking for phonons, with quadrupolar orders favoring non-chiral eigenmodes and timereversal breaking octupolar orders favoring unconventional pseudo-chiral phonons. We compute the temperature dependent phonon matrix Green function using a path integral approach where 'fast' phonon modes sense 'slow' pseudospin fluctuations over a thermal background sampled using Monte Carlo simulations. We propose helicity-resolved Raman spectroscopy of these pseudo-chiral phonons as a probe of hidden octupolar order in quantum materials such as Ba2CaOsO<sup>6</sup> and PrV2Al20.

Phonon chirality has emerged as an important concept in quantum materials, highlighting the strong interplay between phononic and other degrees of freedom such as spin, charge, and orbitals [$$1](#page-5-0)[–6$$](#page-5-1). Chiral phonons are characterized by a nonzero angular momentum and are commonly associated with circularly polarized lattice vibrations [$$7](#page-5-2)[–14$$](#page-5-3), though anomalous rotationless chiral phonons have also been identified [$$15$$](#page-5-4). These phonons have attracted a significant attention due to their strong magnetophononic [$$16–](#page-5-5)[37$$](#page-6-0) and phonomagnetic responses [$$35,](#page-6-1) [38–](#page-6-2)[54$$](#page-6-3).

Chiral phonons inherit signatures of time-reversal symmetry (TRS) breaking in the electronic, orbital, or spin sectors. These signatures manifest as chirality-dependent energy splitting and have been observed across a broad range of materials, including both magnetic [$$23,](#page-5-6) [30,](#page-6-4) [31$$](#page-6-5) and non-magnetic systems [$$17,](#page-5-7) [19$$](#page-5-8). In non-magnetic materials, chirality and mode splitting often arise from underlying nontrivial electronic band topology [$$16,](#page-5-5) [55$$](#page-6-6). In contrast, in magnetic materials, linearly polarized doubly degenerate phonon modes can split into two finite angular momentum-carrying modes either under an external magnetic field [$$23,](#page-5-6) [56,](#page-6-7) [57$$](#page-6-8) or due to intrinsic dipolar magnetic order [$$30–](#page-6-4)[33$$](#page-6-9). Experimentally, these phonons can be detected via helicity-resolved Raman spectroscopy where the two chiralities appear in opposite cross-circular polarization channels [$$10,](#page-5-9) [23,](#page-5-6) [24,](#page-5-10) [30,](#page-6-4) [33$$](#page-6-9). These studies indicate that phonon chirality can serve as a distinct probe of non-trivial band topology or dipolar magnetic orders.

Pseudo-chiral phonons: We begin by introducing the concept of pseudo-chiral phonons. Let us consider a cubic crystal with degenerate E<sup>g</sup> phonon modes (Qx, Qz) ≡ (dx<sup>2</sup>−y<sup>2</sup> , d3<sup>z</sup> <sup>2</sup>−r <sup>2</sup> ). Conventional symmetry breaking associated with lattice distortions can lift this degeneracy while preserving time-reversal symmetry. For instance, a tetragonal distortion will lead to Q<sup>x</sup> and Q<sup>z</sup> becoming non-degenerate phonon eigenmodes. Remarkably, we can also construct time-reversal breaking phonon eigenmodes Q<sup>x</sup> ± iQz. These phonon modes do not result in circular atomic motion and do not carry real angular momentum unlike chiral phonons in dipolar magnets [$$30,](#page-6-4) [33$$](#page-6-9); indeed, the ordinary angular momentum is quenched for E<sup>g</sup> phonons. Instead, these Q<sup>±</sup> modes are characterized by the phase structure shown in Fig. [1](#page-0-4) which endows them with a pseudo-angular momentum (PAM) along the C<sup>3</sup> axis of the cubic crystal, ˆn ∥ [111] (and equivalent cubic body diagonals). This phase structure defines the phonon PAM l along ˆn as

$$C_3^{\hat{n}} Q^{\pm} = e^{\pm i \frac{2\pi}{3} l} Q^{\pm} \quad (1)$$

where l can take values of 0, 1, 2. With this definition, the two pseudo-chiral phonon modes Q<sup>x</sup> ± iQ<sup>z</sup> carry opposite PAM (see SM [$$58$$](#page-6-10) for details and a video of the two modes) and, hence would be visible in opposite crosscircular channels in Raman spectroscopy [$$58$$](#page-6-10). In the remainder of this Letter, we discuss how pseudo-chiral phonons can emerge in the presence of octupolar magnetic order, and how they might shed light on competing multipolar symmetry breaking orders.

Multipole-phonon coupling: As an illustrative example of how diverse multipolar orders can imprint on phonon dynamics, we focus here on competing quadrupolar and octupolar degrees of freedom which emerge for Pr3+ moments on the diamond lattice in PrM2Al<sup>20</sup> (M=Ti,V) [$$77,](#page-7-8) [97–](#page-7-20)[102$$](#page-8-0), and Os6+ or W4+ moments on face-centered cubic (fcc) lattices in Ba2MOsO<sup>6</sup> (M=Ca,Mg) [$$91–](#page-7-17)[95,](#page-7-18) [103–](#page-8-1)[105$$](#page-8-2) and Cs2WCl<sup>6</sup> [$$106–](#page-8-3)[108$$](#page-8-4). The competing multipolar degrees of freedom in these

cubic materials are encoded in local pseudospin-1/2 doublets protected by crystalline point group symmetries, socalled "non-Kramers doublets". The pseudospin operators can be labelled τ = (τx, τy, τz). Here, τ<sup>x</sup> ∝ (J 2 <sup>x</sup> −J 2 y ) and τ<sup>z</sup> ∝ (3J 2 <sup>z</sup> −J(J + 1)) encode quadrupolar moments, while the Ising-like octupole moment is τ<sup>y</sup> ∝ JxJyJ<sup>z</sup> with the overbar denoting operator symmetrization. Here, J is the angular momentum of the ion, with J = 2 for Os6+ or W4+ and J = 4 for Pr3+, which is split by the local crystal field environent. We investigate below the signatures of quadrupolar and octupolar order on E<sup>g</sup> phonon modes (Qx, Qz) ≡ (dx<sup>2</sup>−y<sup>2</sup> , d3<sup>z</sup> <sup>2</sup>−r <sup>2</sup> ) , and show that both types of ordering lift the phonon degeneracy, but in qualitatively distinct ways. For quadrupolar orders, the new phonon eigenmodes are real superpositions of Qx, Q<sup>z</sup> and do not carry PAM. By contrast, for octupolar order, the new phonon eigenmodes are uniform pseudo-chiral superpositions Q<sup>±</sup> = Q<sup>x</sup> ± iQ<sup>z</sup> which carry PAM.

For the face-centered cubic lattice, symmetries dictate the form of the pseudospin Hamiltonian as,

For instance, the Ba2MOsO<sup>6</sup> compounds were proposed to have a ferro-octupolar (FO) ground state ⟨τy⟩ ̸= 0, with J<sup>0</sup> ∼ 1 meV, J1/J<sup>0</sup> ≈ 0.5 and γ ≈ −0.4 [$$109$$](#page-8-5). However, an alternative antiferro-quadrupolar (AFQ) order with staggered ⟨τx,z⟩ ̸= 0 has been suggested as a competing ground state with J1/J<sup>0</sup> ≳ 2 [$$48,](#page-6-17) [95$$](#page-7-18). Given theoretical uncertainties in precisely determining these microscopic exchange couplings, and the absence of smoking gun experiments to distinguish various multipolar orders, the ground state of many of these systems remains debated.

To explore how these pseudospin degrees of freedom imprint on phonons and lead to useful experimental distinctions between these competing orders, we focus on the two degenerate E<sup>g</sup> phonon modes (dx<sup>2</sup>−y<sup>2</sup> , d3<sup>z</sup> <sup>2</sup>−r 2 ) with mode creation operators (b † x , b† z ) respectively. Treating these as Einstein phonons, the phonon Hamiltonian

$$H_{\text{ph}} = \Omega_0 \sum_{\mathbf{r}} \sum_{\ell=x,z} b_{\mathbf{r}\ell}^\dagger b_{\mathbf{r}\ell} \quad (3)$$

where Ω<sup>0</sup> is the E<sup>g</sup> mode frequency (we set <sup>ℏ</sup> = 1). On symmetry grounds, the T -odd octupole will not linearly couple to phonons, however the (τx, τz) quadrupoles can couple linearly to the phonon modes via a symmetry allowed on-site term

$$H_{\text{sp-ph}} = -\lambda \sum_{\mathbf{r}} \sum_{\ell=x,z} (b_{\mathbf{r}\ell} + b_{\mathbf{r}\ell}^{\dagger}) \tau_{\mathbf{r}\ell} \quad (4)$$

where λ is the strength of the spin-phonon coupling. For all the materials in question, the phonon energy scale 3

is much larger than the pseudospin exchange scale; e.g., in Ba2MOsO6, density functional theory studies yield Ω<sup>0</sup> ∼ 60 meV [$$105,](#page-8-2) [110$$](#page-8-6) so Ω<sup>0</sup> ≫ J0, J1. The spinphonon coupling λ can also be estimated from a densityfunctional theory (DFT) calculation; in Ba2CaOsO6, this yields λ ≈ 5 meV [$$48,](#page-6-17) [105$$](#page-8-2). Below, we use a path integral approach to compute the matrix phonon Green function in the limit where 'fast' phonon modes sense the 'slow' pseudospin quantum fluctuations over a thermal background sampled in Monte Carlo (MC) simulations.

Pseudospin-phonon fluctuations: Since the phonon modes have Ω0≫J0, J1, we can treat these 'fast' phonon modes as sensing the 'slow' local pseudospins via the quadrupole-phonon coupling. The pseudospin interactions in Eq. [2](#page-1-0) lead to an effective spin Hamiltonian Hsp = − P <sup>r</sup> h<sup>r</sup> · τ<sup>r</sup> where h<sup>r</sup> is the local Weiss field sensed by τ<sup>r</sup> arising from its neighboring pseudospins. h<sup>r</sup> is nearly uniform in the ordered phase and is randomly distributed in the high temperature paramagnet. For a given background hr, we incorporate leading order spin-wave fluctuations and use this to study the impact on phonon modes, and average the result over the temperature-dependent distribution P({hr}) which we extract using large scale MC simulations.

To study excitations, it is useful to recast the local spin Hamiltonian in terms of Holstein-Primakoff bosons. To focus on transverse spin fluctuations around the local Weiss field direction, we define a local reference frame {τ ′ x , τ ′ y , τ ′ <sup>z</sup>}, via a rotation τ = R(θr, ϕr)· τ ′ where θ<sup>r</sup> and ϕ<sup>r</sup> are the spherical polar and azimuthal angles respectively, chosen such that τ ′ <sup>y</sup> points along hr. In this frame, we define boson operators αr, α† r such that τ ′ <sup>r</sup><sup>y</sup> = 2(S − α † <sup>r</sup>αr), τ ′ <sup>r</sup><sup>x</sup> = √ 2S(α<sup>r</sup> + α † r ), τ ′ <sup>r</sup><sup>z</sup> = i √ 2S(α<sup>r</sup> − α † r ).

This is the key result of our work, which incorporates quantum fluctuations of the pseudospins as well as their thermal fluctuations via Monte Carlo averaging of the pseudospin background. As special cases, let us consider pure octupolar and pure quadrupolar orders. For octupolar order, the pseudospin points along the τ<sup>y</sup> axis, so θ=π/2, ϕ=±π/2, which leads to ∆<sup>x</sup> = ∆<sup>z</sup> = 1, γ = ±2i, and χ = 0. Analytically continuing to the real frequency axis, the imaginary off-diagonal component in G −1 leads to eigenvectors which are pseudo-chiral superpositions Q<sup>x</sup> ± iQ<sup>z</sup> with nonzero PAM. Conversely, when considering quadrupolar order, γ = 0 and the eigenmodes are real superpositions of Qx, Q<sup>z</sup> which do not carry PAM. Turning to the spatial structure of phonon eigenmodes, FO order would host uniform phonon pseudo-chirality while AFO order would exhibit staggered phonon pseudochirality. Similarly, for FQ versus AFQ orders, we expect uniform versus staggered real superpositions of Qx, Qz. With increasing temperature, spatial fluctuations of the pseudospins lead to a filling-in of the spectral gap in A(ω), as the Einstein phonons at different sites in the system sense slightly different environments over the average octupolar order. Sites within an octupolar domain will sense a large FO Weiss field, whereas sites in the vicinity of domain walls where the Weiss field h(r) vanishes will lead to zero splitting. Averaging over these local environments causes a spread in the peak splittings, reflected in the average A(ω) in Fig. [2$](#page-3-0)a). Finally, while sharp spectral peaks signalling a long-range ordered FO phase are absent for T > TFO, there is nevertheless a 'pseudogap' regime where two split broad peaks at Ω±, with spectral weight 10% more than that at ω = 0, persist to a higher temperature T <sup>∗</sup> ∼1.2 TFO (see SM [$$58$$](#page-6-10) for more details).

Fig. [2$](#page-3-0)b) shows the temperature dependent A(ω) incorporating realistic phonon damping 2πη/Ω<sup>0</sup> ∼ 0.07 [$$40,](#page-6-18) [111$$](#page-8-7). Notably, we see that split peaks in the FO phase remain resolvable despite the broadening, which could serve as a signature of FO order. However, the large phonon linewidth masks the 'pseudogap' regime, so A(ω) exhibits a single peak at ω = Ω<sup>0</sup> for T > TFO.

We contrast these spectra in the FO phase with the corresponding spectra in the AFQ phase shown in Figs. [2$](#page-3-0)c) and (d). In this case, the spectral splitting arises from ordering along τx, τ<sup>z</sup> and involves breaking of point group symmetry rather than time-reversal symmetry. In contrast to the FO case, the mode splitting in the AFQ phase is governed by unequal diagonal terms ∆<sup>x</sup> and ∆<sup>z</sup> in G −1 (r, iνn) in Eqs. [10](#page-2-0) and [12.](#page-2-0) This leads to one mode at Ω<sup>0</sup> and the second mode shifted downward (redshifted) to Ω<sup>0</sup> − ∆Ω with the mode splitting ∆Ω ≈ 4λ <sup>2</sup>hr/Ω 2 <sup>0</sup> ≈ 0.4 meV since h<sup>r</sup> = (6−2γ)J<sup>1</sup> ≈ 6.8J<sup>1</sup> deep in the AFQ phase. This mode splitting in the AFQ phase is parametrically smaller than that in the FO phase, being ∝1/Ω 2 0 . As a result, while a very weak splitting is visible at low damping in Fig. [2$](#page-3-0)c), there is no visible peak splitting in the AFQ phase with realistic phonon damping in Fig. [2$](#page-3-0)d) even for T ≪ TAFQ, but we observe a gradual T-dependent shift of the central peak.

The above results show that there can be important qualitative and quantitative differences in the phonon spectral function in the FO and AFQ phases. Probing the phonons may thus provide a route to discern between these different multipolar orders in candidate materials.

Discussion: In this work, we propose a new probe for multipolar magnetic order which leverages symmetrybreaking imprint on phonons. While recent works on dipolar magnets [$$30,](#page-6-4) [33$$](#page-6-9) have observed the signatures of phonon chirality and mode splitting via Raman spectroscopy, the phonon signatures of multipolar magnetism remain unexplored. Using a path-integral approach, we show a FO order induced pseudochiral phonons with a splitting ∼ 1 meV, similar to values reported for dipolar magnets [$$30,](#page-6-4) [33$$](#page-6-9). These pseudo-chiral phonons can be observed via Raman spectroscopy in the back scattering geometry with light propagating along the cubic [111] axes, with different intensities in different circularly polarized channels (see [$$58$$](#page-6-10) for details). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first such prediction, which does not rely on magnetic field, net magnetization, non-trivial band topology, or even on the circular motion of ions!

Interestingly, while our results for the normalized Tdependent mode splitting qualitatively tracks, but is not simply proportional to, the FO order as highlighted in Fig[.4.](#page-4-1) We trace this to the fact that the splitting in the FO phase is governed by the γ<sup>r</sup> off-diagonal term in G −1 (r, iνn), which tracks only the direction but not the magnitude of the local Weiss fields. A similar discrepancy between mode splitting and the magnetic order parameter in seen in recent experimental results on the ferrimagnetic insulator Fe1.75Zn0.25Mo3O<sup>8</sup> [$$33$$](#page-6-9). We believe that our approach can capture such effects as arising from spatial non-uniformities of the order parameter.

In summary, our phonon-based proposal offers an accessible route to probing octupolar orders with optical spectroscopy, complementing other tools such as magnetostriction [$$82,](#page-7-10) [101$$](#page-8-8), polarized neutron scattering [$$112$$](#page-8-9), NMR [$$104,](#page-8-10) [105,](#page-8-2) [113,](#page-8-11) [114$$](#page-8-12), and X-ray diffraction [$$87$$](#page-7-14). Using such complementary probes could shed valuable light on complex multipolar orders in a wide variety of quantum materials. Moreover, the theoretical approach used here can accommodate more general TRS breaking scenarios and might find applications in a wide variety of antiferromagnets, altermagnets [$$63,](#page-6-15) [65,](#page-7-24) [115$$](#page-8-13), or even in the pseudo-gap phase of cuprates where thermal Hall measurements have hinted at chiral phonons [$$116,](#page-8-14) [117$$](#page-8-15). ## I. E<sup>g</sup> PHONONS IN Ba2MgOsO<sup>6</sup>

For the purpose of calculating displacement vectors associated with E<sup>g</sup> phonons, we consider double perovskite Ba2MgOsO6. It crystallizes in a face-centered cubic (FCC) structure with space group Fm¯3m (No. 225). This structure is characterized by the rock-salt ordering of the B-site cations (Mg2+ and Os6+), embedded within a framework of corner-sharing oxygen octahedra. The parent perovskite structure has a general formula of A2BB'O6, where:

- The A-site is occupied by Ba2+ cations.
- The B and B' sites are occupied by Mg2+ and Os6+ respectively, in a rock-salt type arrangement.
- Oxygen atoms form a three-dimensional octahedral network around each B-site cation.

The atomic positions in the unit cell are described by the following Wyckoff positions:

- Ba2+: occupies the 8c site at coordinates ( <sup>1</sup> 4 , 4 , 1 4 )
- Mg2+: occupies the 4a site at coordinates (0, 0, 0)
- Os6+: occupies the 4b site at coordinates ( <sup>1</sup> 2 , 1 2 , 2 )
- O2-: occupies the 24e site at coordinates (x, 0, 0), where x ≈ 0.24. These oxygen ions are shown by six different colors in Fig. [S1.](#page-11-0)

## Displacement pattern for E<sup>g</sup> modes

The E<sup>g</sup> mode involves distortions of the oxygen octahedra, particularly planar stretching and compressing motions. We consider a basis consisting of six oxygen ions located at the positions (x, 0, 0), (−x, 0, 0), (0, x, 0), (0, −x, 0), (0, 0, x), and (0, 0, −x), which undergo displacements in different directions. The remaining eighteen oxygen ions in the unit cell can be generated by translating these six basis atoms by the vectors (0, 1 2 , 2 ), ( <sup>1</sup> 2 , 0, 1 2 ), and ( <sup>1</sup> 2 , 1 2 , 0). The corresponding displacements of these translated atoms follow from those of the original six.

To clearly illustrate the displacement pattern, the six oxygen atoms in each Os-centered octahedron are shown in six different colors in Fig. [S1.](#page-11-0) Using the transformation properties of the irreducible representation Eg, we obtain the following displacement pattern:

$$Q(E_g^a) = \frac{1}{2} (\hat{x}, -\hat{x}, -\hat{y}, \hat{y}, 0, 0) \quad (1)$$

$$Q(E_g^b) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{3}} (-\hat{x}, \hat{x}, -\hat{y}, \hat{y}, 2\hat{z}, -2\hat{z}) \quad (2)$$

These displacement patterns are shown in Fig. [S1.](#page-11-0) The most interesting feature to note here is that both components exhibit linear motion in the same direction. This implies that the chiral superposition Q(E<sup>a</sup> g ) ± iQ(E<sup>b</sup> g ) cannot result in circular motion. However, it has been recently shown in Ref. It is defined in terms of the phase picked up by the phonon eigenstate under three-fold rotation and has been commonly employed to study chiral valley phonons [$$2$$](#page-15-1). In a crystal with m-fold rotational symmetry about ˆn axis, for a phonon mode ν with momentum q, PAM l p <sup>ν</sup><sup>q</sup> points along ˆn is defined as

$$C_m^{\hat{n}} \mathbf{u}_\nu \mathbf{q} e^{i\mathbf{R}_{\alpha l} \cdot \mathbf{q}} = e^{-i\frac{2\pi}{m} \hat{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}_\nu^p \mathbf{q}} \mathbf{u}_\nu \mathbf{q} e^{i\mathbf{R}_{\alpha l} \cdot \mathbf{q}}, \quad (3)$$

and can take values of l p <sup>ν</sup><sup>q</sup> = 0, ...,(m − 1), and Rαl is the position vector for atom α in unit cell l. The phase factor can arise from a rotation of the displacement vector uν<sup>q</sup> directly (intracell, spin PAM), or from a rotation of the nonlocal part, e iR<sup>j</sup> ·q , (intercell, orbital PAM). This quantized PAM arises at points in the Brillouin zone that respect n-fold rotational symmetry.

In the current scenario of E<sup>g</sup> modes in a double-perovskite crystal, the point group of the crystal has three-fold rotational symmetry about each diagonal of the cubic unit cell. We can define PAM according to the transformation of chiral E<sup>g</sup> modes under a three-fold rotation about the following four-diagonals

$$\hat{n}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(\hat{x} + \hat{y} + \hat{z}), \hat{n}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(\hat{x} + \hat{y} - \hat{z}), \hat{n}_3 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(\hat{x} + \hat{y} - \hat{z}), \hat{n}_4 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(\hat{x} - \hat{y} - \hat{z}). \quad (4)$$

The chiral eigenmodes are given by:

$$Q^{\pm} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} (Q(E_g^a) \pm iQ(E_g^b)) = \frac{i}{\sqrt{3}} \left( \hat{x} e^{\pm i \frac{4\pi}{3}}, -\hat{x} e^{\pm i \frac{4\pi}{3}}, \hat{y} e^{\pm i \frac{2\pi}{3}}, -\hat{y} e^{\pm i \frac{2\pi}{3}}, \hat{z}, -\hat{z} \right) \quad (5)$$

and they satisfy:

$$C_3^{\hat{n}_1} Q^\pm = e^{\pm i \frac{2\pi}{3}} Q^\pm, C_3^{\hat{n}_2} Q^\pm = e^{\mp i \frac{2\pi}{3}} Q^\pm, C_3^{\hat{n}_3} Q^\pm = e^{\mp i \frac{2\pi}{3}} Q^\pm, C_3^{\hat{n}_4} Q^\pm = e^{\pm i \frac{2\pi}{3}} Q^\pm \quad (6)$$

which indicates that Q<sup>±</sup> carry opposite angular momentum when measured about one of the diagonal ˆn<sup>i</sup> of the cubic unit cell. Additionally, the sign of this pseudo-angular momentum also depends on the direction of rotation axis. This kind of PAM is important for angular momentum conservation in Raman scattering and selection rules [$$2–](#page-15-1)[6$$](#page-15-2).

### Raman tensor and selection rules

In the (x, y, z) crystal frame, Raman tensor for E<sup>g</sup> modes is given by:

$$R(\mathcal{E}_g^a) = \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{3}a & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -\sqrt{3}a & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad R(\mathcal{E}_g^b) = \begin{pmatrix} -a & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -a & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2a \end{pmatrix}, \quad (7)$$

where a ≡ a(ω) is a function of frequency. As discussed above, a chiral superposition of these modes carry PAM along the diagonal of the cube. In order to detect the angular momentum in Raman scattering, we can consider light propagating along ˆn1, i.e [111] direction. Next, we can define a set of three cartesian vectors with their axis aligned along ˆn1:

$$\hat{e}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1, -1, 0), \quad \hat{e}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}(1, 1, -2), \quad \hat{e}_3 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(1, 1, 1). \quad (8)$$

In this basis Raman tensor for two components of the given E<sup>g</sup> mode takes the following form:

$$R'(\mathcal{E}_g^a) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & a & \sqrt{2}a \\ a & 0 & 0 \\ \sqrt{2}a & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad R'(\mathcal{E}_g^b) = \begin{pmatrix} -a & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & a & -\sqrt{2}a \\ 0 & -\sqrt{2}a & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \quad (9)$$

resulting in the following Raman tensors for Q<sup>±</sup> modes:

$$R'(Q^+) = \begin{pmatrix} -ia & a & \sqrt{2}a \\ a & ia & -i\sqrt{2}a \\ \sqrt{2}a & -i\sqrt{2}a & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad R'(Q^-) = \begin{pmatrix} ia & 0 & \sqrt{2}a \\ 0 & -ia & i\sqrt{2}a \\ \sqrt{2}a & i\sqrt{2}a & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \quad (10)$$
